Inside the cold-water data hall that cut a city's cooling bill in half

A converted harbour warehouse uses seawater loops instead of air conditioning, and the numbers are surprising.

By Idris Kamara · November 6, 2026

On a grey morning in the made-up city of Northmere, the loudest thing in the old harbour warehouse is a pump. Behind it, forty rows of servers hum quietly in a hall that is cooled by a loop of cold seawater instead of rooftop air conditioners.

The operator, Tidewell Compute, says the design cut its cooling energy by roughly half compared with its previous site. The warm water that leaves the building is sent on to heat a public swimming pool next door.

Heat that finds a second job

Engineers had to solve less glamorous problems first: salt, algae and a council that wanted proof the harbour would not warm up. Independent sensors now publish water temperature every ten minutes on a public page.

Not everyone is convinced. A local sailing club worries about noise from the pumps, and an energy analyst points out that the approach only works for sites close to deep, cold water.

Tidewell plans a second hall next year. "If it works here, it will work in a dozen other harbour towns," said site manager Elena Vos.
